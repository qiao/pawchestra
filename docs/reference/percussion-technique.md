# Percussion technique: how professional percussionists hold and move the mallets

This file is the target of the motion of the percussion players of the app. It describes the
grip of the mallets, the sticks, the beater, and the cymbals, their directions between strikes and
at a strike, the stroke, and the playing spot of professional percussionists, with a source for
each fact. Sections 1 to 4 give the timpani, and sections 5 to 9 the instruments of the percussion
section.

**Upkeep.** This file is current. A change of the percussion motion of the app updates section
10 "Use in the app" and each fact that it finds wrong, in the same commit. Each new fact gets its
source.

**Source marks.** Each fact names its source by a number of section 11. "(abstract)" marks a fact
from an abstract or a search summary, because the full text did not open. "(secondary)" marks a
fact that another source quotes. "(derived)" marks a value that this file calculates from the
values of its sources. "(photo)" marks a fact that a photo of the source shows, with no text.
A fact with no mark comes from the text of the source.

## Terms

- **Fulcrum.** The point where the hand pinches the shaft, between the thumb and a finger. The
  mallet turns around it.
- **Butt end.** The end of the shaft opposite to the head.
- **Playing spot.** The place on the head where the mallet strikes.
- **V.** The angle between the two mallets, seen from above.

## 1. The grip

- The fulcrum is between the thumb and the index finger, and the back fingers wrap the shaft
  loosely [1]. Wikipedia puts it between the thumb and the middle finger [8 (secondary)].
- The thumb lies flat along the shaft, and its nail points to the head [1]. In the French grip,
  the thumb points down the shaft toward the head [3]. Thus the head leaves the front of the fist,
  at the side of the thumb and the index finger, and the shaft lies along the thumb side of the
  hand (derived).
- The butt end sticks out about 0.5 to 1 inch (13 to 25 mm) behind the hand [1]. It stays loose
  in the palm, with some play between the butt and the hand [6].
- The fulcrum is about one third of the way up the shaft [12 (abstract)].

## 2. The three grips

| Grip | The hands | The stroke | The V between the mallets |
|---|---|---|---|
| German | The backs of the hands up, the thumbs in, toward each other [1] | From the wrist [1] | About 90° in front of the player [1] |
| French | The thumbs up [1] [3] | Mostly a turn of the forearm, as a turn of a door knob [1] [3] | About 20° to 30° [1]. The mallets are almost parallel [4] |
| American | Between the two: the thumbs and the palms at about 45° [1] [2] | A mix | Between 20° and 90° [1] |

Thus each mallet points forward and in, 10° to 45° from the straight forward direction (derived
from the V).

## 3. The mallet between strikes and at a strike

- The shaft is 10° to 25° from the drum head [3]. Peters gives about 10° above the head [2].
- The mallets do not go below the level [5].
- In the set position before the first note, the mallets are about one inch (2.5 cm) above the
  drum head, parallel to the floor, and the forearm slopes down about 10° to 15° to the wrist [41
  (a search summary only)].
- A general stroke is a smooth down-up motion that starts with the mallet above the head, and
  the player lifts the mallet head up at once after the attack [40]. Louder notes fall from a
  greater height [42]. The heights of 12 inches [5] and 18 inches [7] are drills.
- The upper arm hangs beside the body, and the elbow bends so that the mallet reaches the head
  [3]. The shoulders do not lift [1].
- The wrist has a small bend, which lets it turn [4].
- A timpanist can stand to play [11], or sit on a stool [4]. A stool can also be only a support to
  lean against [3].

## 4. The stroke and the playing spot

- The mallet lifts out of the head at once, because a long contact damps the tone [1]. The
  upstroke starts before the strike, as if the player pulls the sound out of the drum [3]. The
  stroke is a toss with a natural rebound [5].
- The stroke turns the wrist, and the arm moves little [4] [10]. The arm moves more in loud
  play [10].
- The playing spot is about 2 to 4 inches (5 to 10 cm) in from the rim [2] [5], or one third of
  the way from the rim to the middle, about 4 inches [3] [9]. A spot 6 inches or more in gives a
  dead tone [5].
- The spot is directly in front of the player [7]. The smallest drum plays nearer the rim, at
  about 2 inches [7].
- The two mallets strike about 6 inches (15 cm) apart [4], in the same area of the head [7].
- Timpani rolls are single strokes from hand to hand. A student starts with eighth notes at 80
  beats each minute, then plays sixteenth notes and thirty-second notes. The roll does not have
  to be very fast, because a roll that is too fast sounds choked, and the stick height does not
  change with the speed [53]. A roll of thirty-second notes at 80 beats each minute has about 10.7
  strokes each second, about 5.3 for each hand (derived).
- A roll on a low note and a large drum is slower than a roll on a high note and a small drum,
  because a loose head vibrates longer [53] [54].
- The player muffles a head with the middle, ring, and little fingers, and keeps the mallet
  between the thumb and the index finger [55] [56] [58]. The fingers touch the head lightly in the
  playing spot, and do not press or slap [55] [56] [57].
- The player muffles at the rests of the music, until the sound stops or the next note needs the
  hand [56]. A rest does not always need a muffle [58 (secondary)]. One method muffles the last
  note at the attack of the new note [57], and another muffles a half note on its third beat and a
  quarter note on its second beat [59]. The most recent drums come first [56] [59].

## 5. The bass drum

- The player stands behind the drum, with the left foot between the legs of the stand, so that
  one hand reaches both heads and the beater stays parallel to the head [13].
- The right hand holds one beater, about one third up from the butt, loosely, with the thumb on
  the side, as in the legato snare grip [13] [14].
- The playing spot is off the center: halfway between the edge and the center [15], one third in
  from the rim [16], or just below the center, in front of the knee of the player [14].
- The stroke uses the wrist and the forearm, with an upstroke, and the upper arm for loud notes
  [15] [16]. The beater rebounds and moves away, so that the head rings [13].
- The free hand muffles the head with the open palm and the fingers, mostly in the rests [13]
  [14]. A single note often needs no muffling [13]. The player muffles only for a rest, a note
  length, the style, or the conductor, and in a march muffles in the rests and not at the
  strikes [13]. A knee on the playing side can muffle the playing head, and the free hand the back
  head [58].
- The frame tilts the drum from vertical to horizontal. Rolls take a tilt of about 45°, but the
  drum does not lie flat [14] [28].
- The player focuses on the movement of the conductor, which gives the pulse, and moves to mimic
  it, because the percussion section is the farthest section from the conductor [68].
- In the photos of the set-up and the grip, the hand holds the mallet at the near rim of the
  head, with the mallet along the head toward its middle, and the back of the hand away from the
  head [13 (photo)].
- An orchestral bass drum is roughly 40 inches (1.02 m) wide [28]. A concert model of Yamaha is
  36 by 16 inches [43 (a search summary only)]. A concert beater for the orchestra is 419 mm
  long, with a head of 69.8 mm [44].

## 6. The snare drum

- The head is about 3 to 4 inches (a hand span) below the navel [17]. The drum stands level [17].
- The fulcrum is one third up from the butt, between the thumb and the forefinger. The back
  fingers wrap the stick loosely, and about two thirds of the stick leaves the front of the hand
  [17] [18].
- The palms are nearly flat, and the tips of the sticks make a V of 60° to 90° [17] [18] [19].
- The wrists are about 1 inch above the head, so the sticks are almost parallel to the floor, and
  slant down a little at a strike [17].
- The sticks strike near the middle of the head, about 1 inch above it on the line out from the
  body [18], or a little off the middle [9].
- The elbows are about a fist from the body, the upper arms hang mostly still, and the wrist
  makes the stroke [17]. A full stroke lifts the tip about 8 inches [18].
- Between entrances, the player holds the sticks in a rest position: level and across the body
  in front of the waist, side by side, one in each hand [36 (photo)]. The ready position holds the
  tips 1 to 2 inches above the head, and the sticks can go back to it in a rest of the music
  [36]. No object goes on the drum head [36].
- A drumline holds a set position between passages: the two sticks parallel, facing opposite
  directions, in front of the belly and about one inch above the rim nearest to the body. The
  players go to the playing position two beats before an entrance, and back to the set position
  one beat after the end of a passage [37].

## 7. The crash cymbals and the suspended cymbal

- Hand cymbals of all weights are 14 to 22 inches wide [23]: 0.356 to 0.559 m (derived).
- The thumb and the forefinger hold the strap near the bell. The hand does not go through the
  strap [15] [20] [23].
- The plates are in front of the middle of the chest, with their edges about 3 inches from the
  body [21], or with the top edges at about eye level [15] [20].
- The plates are not vertical. The left plate can lie almost level under the left hand, with the
  elbow near the stomach, and the right plate comes in at about 20° to it [20]. Other sources give
  about 45°, with the plate of the strong hand on top [23], and 5° to 45° from vertical [16].
- The right plate leads and comes down onto the left plate, one edge first, as a flam [20] [21]
  [23]. Better players move both plates [20] [22].
- After a long crash, the plates keep moving and turn out to let the sound go to the hall [21],
  or go up above the shoulders [22]. A fast note takes less of this follow move [21].
- Before a crash, the player holds the plates in front of the middle of the chest, about 4 inches
  apart [21]. The non-dominant plate stays still, and the dominant plate comes in at 35° to 45°
  with its tip 1 to 2 inches down the inside of the other plate [60]. The plates meet as a
  glancing blow, as a flam, so that the air between them escapes [21] [60]. A large slice up or
  down after the crash is a fault [60]. A soft crash has a smaller angle between the plates, but
  the plates are still not parallel [61]. A short note stops against the chest or the stomach [15]
  [20] [22]. No concert source gives the rest place. A marching method holds the plates at the
  sides, almost vertical [24].
- A part marked "with stick" or "with mallet" plays on a suspended cymbal: one cymbal on a stand,
  struck with a stick or a beater [23] [32].
- A suspended cymbal roll uses medium to soft yarn mallets. A cymbal of 17 inches or less takes a
  lighter mallet [30]. Suspended cymbals for orchestras and bands are sold at 18 inches
  [33 (abstract)].
- The roll plays near the edge, about half an inch in, with the left and the right mallets
  opposite each other [21] [30] [32]. Another source tells the player not to play exactly
  opposite, because the plate then rocks [31].
- The stroke is legato, as on a marimba bar, and comes off the cymbal at the end of the roll
  [30]. Single notes play at the very edge with a legato stroke [31].
- The suspended cymbal hangs flat, a little above the height of the waist [38].
- No source gives where a suspended cymbal player holds the mallets between entrances. The
  bongos, played with snare sticks on a stand, take the ready position and the rest position of
  the snare drum [36] [36 (photo)].

## 8. The triangle and the tambourine

- The left hand holds the triangle by a clip with a loop, at eye level, and the right hand holds
  the beater [15] [25].
- The beater strikes the middle of the bottom side [9], or the outside near the bottom [16], with
  a motion that pushes the bottom side away [15]. A strike at 45° gives a darker sound [9].
- The fingers of the left hand mute the triangle [16] [25]. Interlochen mutes it with the back of
  the hand and the fingers, and holds it in the non-dominant hand on the ring finger and the thumb
  [25].
- A triangle roll moves the beater fast between two edges, the two top edges or the two bottom
  edges [25]. A roll can play in each closed corner, and not in the open corner [63]. One teacher
  finds the bottom corner best [64 (secondary)].
- The left hand holds the tambourine by its frame, at or a little below the chest, tilted in at
  about 45°, and the right hand strikes it [15] [16] [65]. Between uses it lies on a padded trap
  table, and a soft fast passage can play on the table [65] [66]. No source of this file gives a
  tambourine on a stand, or a table for a quick change of instruments.
- Single notes: three bunched fingers at the middle for mf to f, a loose fist or the flat of the
  knuckles for f to ff, and the fingertips near the rim for pp to mp [65]. Another source strikes
  1 to 2 inches from the edge [63]. A louder passage holds the tambourine higher [66].
- A long note is a shake roll: a turn of the forearm, as a door knob [65] [66]. A soft roll is a
  thumb roll or a finger roll [65].
- A common mistake of an orchestrator is too little time for a player to change the instrument or
  to take new beaters [67]. No source of this file gives a time for a change.

## 9. The xylophone

- The player stands with the feet about the width of the shoulders apart, and moves the body from
  side to side behind the notes, with few steps [26] [27].
- The bars are at the height where the wrist bends naturally, as for a snare drum. The forearms
  are a little below parallel to the floor [26] [27].
- The grip is the matched grip of the snare drum [18] [26]. The mallets make a symmetrical V, and
  do not cross [27]. No source gives the angle.
- A mallet strikes a natural bar at its middle, and a sharp bar at its edge [9], the edge near the
  player (search summary of [29]).
- The wrists stay low, and the elbows move forward to reach the back row [27].
- A scale plays with alternate strokes, from either hand, and the hands also alternate on the
  sharp bars of a chromatic scale. An ascending chromatic scale takes no double strokes [34].
- About 90% of mallet technique is alternate single strokes. Where full alternation would make
  the hands cross, the player puts in a double stroke, two strokes of one hand, for a small
  interval, and only where it is necessary [35].
- George Hamilton Green held the left mallet in front of the right mallet, the standard
  set-up of two mallets for decades. In his tremolos on the upper manual, the left mallet played
  in the middle of the bar, and the right mallet on the extreme near end [39].
- Bob Becker plays with the palms down and the hands at most one inch above the keyboard. His
  stroke is two or three inches high at each dynamic, made almost only with the wrist [39].
- Most teachers set the stick height by the dynamic: a louder note starts higher above the bars
  [62]. The player moves each hand to its next note as soon as it has played [62].

### The elephant at the timpani

The app follows these facts:

- **The grip (sections 1 and 2).** The palm of the stump paw of the Tripo elephant faces down,
  as in the German grip, and the shaft crosses the lower side of the closed stump, as through a
  fist (`TIMPANI.malletSpotL`, `malletSpotR`, `malletTilt`). The end of the stump closes 20° onto
  the shaft (`PercussionHold.curl`), the head leaves the front of the fist, and the butt end
  sticks out 2 cm to 3 cm behind the paw (`TIMPANI.malletButt`).
- **The V (section 2).** Each mallet points forward and 27° to 34° in, a V of about 60°
  (`PercussionHold.home`).
- **The elbows (section 3).** Each upper arm hangs near the side, and the elbow points down and a
  little out (`poleL`, `poleR`). Elbows farther out show a gap between the arm and the body from
  the back.
- **The set position (section 3).** Between strikes, the wrist lifts the head about one inch over
  the drum head, and the shaft slopes 13° to 17° down (`MalletAcross.pitch`).
- **The stroke (sections 3 and 4).** The head lifts before the note, comes down at its full speed
  at the note start, and rebounds (`STRIKE_MOTION`). The wrist turns the head up by 35° to 70°,
  and a louder note lifts higher (`MalletAcross.peak`), as louder notes fall from a greater
  height [42]. The arm moves little. A strike within 0.75 s of the strike before it is a
  legato stroke: the head lifts at once after the strike before, as a real player lifts the
  mallet at once after the attack [40], and waits at its top (`STRIKE_MOTION.legatoSec`). After a
  longer rest, the head lifts one beat before the note, at most 1 s before it
  (`STRIKE_MOTION.leadSec`), as a drumline goes to the playing position before an entrance [37].
- **The muffle (section 4).** After a note with a rest of at least 0.5 s after it, the paw that
  struck the note lays the end of the closed paw lightly on its head, for at most 0.3 s, and goes
  back before the next note, as a real player muffles at a rest [56] (`FingersDamp`). The spine
  leans only 3°, so that the trunk stays out of the kettles and the body stays over its feet.
  The mallet keeps its strike direction, and the wrist lifts its head off the drum, so the mallet
  stays in the paw [55] [56]. A pause in a muffle keeps the paw on the head. The upper body does
  not turn. The paw moves to the muffle and back at most at 1.2 m/s (`FingersDamp.speed`), so a
  short rest after a note has no muffle, as a rest does not always need one [58 (secondary)].

The app does not follow these facts:

- **The height of the snare drum for a thin body (section 6).** A thin recast body keeps the
  height of the badger (`PercussionHold.fit`), also when the head is far below its elbows. Only
  a body whose belly needs the drum forward sets it higher. With the head below the elbows of each
  body, the sticks of the thin wolf and deer went into the raised drum in the lowering.
- **The fingers (section 1).** The elephant paw has no fingers to wrap the shaft.
- **The place of the muffle (section 4).** The paw touches the head between the playing spot and
  the near rim, at least 5 cm in from the rim, as far toward the spot as the short arm reaches
  (`FingersDamp.reach`). The spine leans 3° over the kettles. No source gives a lean. From an
  upright body,
  the lowest point of the paw stayed 11 cm to 15 cm over the head. Kettles of 0.92 m, in the
  0.84 m to 0.94 m of an adjustable kettle, and 6 cm nearer, let the paw reach the head, but they
  moved the left arm past the margins of the arm gate (paw twist p95 170° against 136°). A lean
  of 17° put the forearms up to 1.8 cm into the belly.
- **The set position (section 3).** Between strikes, the shaft slopes 13° to 17° down. A real
  player holds it parallel to the floor, but the stump paws stand over the heads, and a lower grip
  put them into the kettles. At a strike, the shaft slopes 24° to 26° down, a little more than the 10° to
  25° of a real player. Each paw keeps the roll that has no side bend at the strike
  (`PercussionHold.strikeRoll`), so the shaft stays in the paw.
- **The roll (section 4).** A roll on one kettle stays in one hand, because each hand plays only
  the front kettle on its side (`PercussionHold.pair`). The hand strikes on some notes of the roll,
  at most 6 times each second (`STRIKE_MOTION.handRollSec`, `thinRolls()`), the rate of one hand
  in a real roll.
- **The playing spot (section 4).** Each mallet strikes farther in from the near rim
  (`PercussionHold.rim`), because the short arms hold the paws above the near rims. Each hand
  plays only the front kettle on its side (`PercussionHold.pair`), so the two mallets never strike
  one head together. The spots move toward the middle of the player (`offsetL`, `offsetR`).

### The badger at the other instruments

The app follows these facts:

- **The grip (section 1).** The curled claws (`GRIP_CURL`) hold the shaft on the palm side, and
  the head leaves the front of the fist at the thumb side (`PAW_SPOT`, `PAW_TILT`). The butt end
  sticks out behind the paw (`malletButt()`, `MALLET_BUTT`). Each paw keeps the turn of the hand
  of the player (`PercussionHold.keepPaw`), so the claws stay closed on the shaft
  (`pnpm wind-grip`).
- **The bass drum (section 5).** The beater has the size of a real one [44], and the drum the depth
  of a real one [43], at the scale of the badger, its head top over the mean height of an adult [45]
  (`BASS_DRUM`). The badger stands behind the drum, and the right paw holds the beater at the near
  rim, one third up from the butt. At the strike, the beater lies along the head, as the photos of
  [13] show, and strikes near one third in from the rim [16]. The left arm hangs while the badger
  plays, because a single note often needs no muffling and a march muffles in the rests, not at the
  strikes [13]. After a note with a rest of at least 0.5 s after it, the free left paw lays its open
  palm and fingers on the far head and goes back before the next note, as a player muffles the back
  head with the free hand [13] [58] (`PalmDamp`). The right upper arm hangs beside the body with the
  elbow down and back (section 3, `poleR`), so that the round rebuilt arm stays out of the belly.
  The neck and the head turn back up by the lean of the spine, so that the badger looks ahead to
  the conductor over the drum, as a player focuses on the movement of the conductor [68]
  (`PercussionHold.headUp`).
- **The snare drum (section 6).** The head is at about the height of the wrists (`SNARE_DRUM`).
  The sticks make a V and slant down a little at a strike. Between strikes, each tip stays about
  4 cm above the head, with the stick almost level, as in the ready position
  (`MalletAcross.pitch`). Before a strike, the tip lifts higher for a louder note: about 11 cm to
  15 cm above the head, and about 10 cm in a roll (`StrikeSizes.lift`, `MalletAcross.peak`). At a
  strike, the ball at the tip touches the head (`SNARE_DRUM.tip`). Each paw holds its stick one
  third up from the butt (`SNARE_DRUM.malletButt`). The stick is 0.45 m long, a little longer
  than a real stick, because a shorter stick in front of the grip put the tips out of the reach
  of the short arms. The elbows point down and a little forward, about a fist from the body [17]
  (`poleL`, `poleR`): the upper arms stand about 20° out from the side of the body, as the
  hanging arms do. At rest, the sticks lie across the body in front of the waist, 3° and 8° from level,
  with the tips 3.5 cm and 6.8 cm above the head and about 1 cm in from the near rim, as the set
  position of a drumline [37] (`PercussionHold.set`). The two heights keep the sticks 3 cm apart. The sticks also go there in a long rest of the
  music, of at least 6 beats: one beat after the last strike, and back from 2 beats to 1 beat
  before the next strike, as the set position of a drumline [37] (`SET_MOTION`). The mallets of
  the suspended cymbal do the same.
  A deep recast body sets the drum higher and further forward (`PercussionHold.fit`): the head
  toward the height below its elbows [17], and the near rim in front of its belly, so that its
  paws stay in front of the body.
- **The suspended cymbal (section 7).** The badger plays the cymbal parts on a suspended cymbal
  (`SpeciesParams.cymbalForm`, `SUSPENDED_CYMBAL`). Its short arms cannot hold hand cymbals of the
  smallest real size out of its round body. The paws strike in turn near the edge, opposite each
  other. Between strikes, each ball stays about 3.5 cm above the plate, with the shaft almost
  level, as the ready position of the snare drum. Before a strike, it lifts higher for a louder
  note: about 6 cm to 13 cm (`SUSPENDED_CYMBAL_HOLD.across`). After a note with a rest of at
  least 0.5 s after it, the paw that did not strike goes to the near rim, its fingers touch the
  edge, and the spine leans 9° over the cymbal, as a player stops the ring of a short note
  [15] [20] [22] (`EdgeDamp`).
- **The tambourine (section 8).** A run of tambourine notes with a rest of at least 2.7 s
  before it and after it is held in the left paw by its frame, in front of the chest, and the
  right paw strikes the head with the palm to the head (`TAMBOURINE_HOLD`). A soft note strikes
  near the rim with open fingertips, a middle note with bunched fingers, and a loud note with a
  loose fist, nearer to the middle [65]. A run of notes faster than eight each second is a roll:
  a loud roll turns the left forearm to and fro, as a door knob, and a soft roll moves the right
  paw along the rim on the head, as a thumb roll [65] [66]. Between uses, the tambourine and the
  beater lie on a padded trap table [65] [66], and the triangle hangs from the hook of a stand.
  In the rest, the paws put down one instrument and take the other (`SWITCH_MOTION`).
- **The triangle (section 8).** The left paw holds the upper end of the clip between the tip of
  the thumb and the curled fingers, with the thumb down, and the clip and the triangle hang below
  the paw in a loop of cord [15] [25] (`TRIANGLE_CLIP`). The paw stays in line with its forearm,
  and the triangle keeps its hang below the pinch (`trianglePaw()`). A single note strikes the side
  across from the open corner [63]. Before a louder note, the beater turns further back
  (`MalletAcross.peak`). A roll moves the beater fast between the two sides of the closed lower
  corner, from inside [25] [63] (`TRIANGLE_ROLL`, `triangleRolls()`).
- **The xylophone (section 9).** The body moves from side to side behind the notes with the feet
  in place (`PercussionHold.side`), and the head turns back so the eyes stay level. A fast strike
  takes the other paw, and a strike that would cross the paws takes a double stroke [35]
  (`PercussionHold.pass`, `PercussionHold.gap`). Between strikes, each head stays about 4 cm over
  its bar, and before a strike it lifts higher for a louder note [62] (`MalletAcross.peak`). A
  mallet strikes a sharp bar near its edge near the player.
- **The hand cymbals of a recast body (section 7).** The plates tilt from vertical, and the right
  plate comes down onto the left plate. The right plate comes in at an angle of 15° to the left
  plate, so that the top rims meet first, as a flam [20] [21] [23], and the plates are not
  parallel [61]. The rims meet a little offset and slide on past each other, as a glancing blow
  [21] [60]. A louder crash meets higher. After a crash, the plates turn
  out to the front and up and rise, less after a soft crash and not after a fast one [21] [23]. At
  the end of a note before a rest, the plates go against the chest [20] [22] (`CRASH_MOTION`,
  `DampRule`).

The app does not follow these facts:

- **The full stroke of the snare drum (section 6).** The tip of a loud note lifts about 15 cm,
  less than the 8 inches of a full stroke, because the size of a gesture grows only up to two
  times from a soft note to a loud note (`gestureSize()`), and a soft note must stay low.
- **The lean over the bass drum (section 5).** The spine leans over the drum
  (`PercussionHold.lean`), so that the short arm can bend.
- **The width of the bass drum (section 5).** The drum is 32 inches wide (`BASS_DRUM.radius`),
  less than the 36 inches of [43] and the 40 inches of [28], and its middle stands lower. With
  the drum of 36 inches, the snout of the leaning badger touched the top hoop, and a drum further
  forward or lower put the beater off its spot, because the short arm reaches no farther. No
  source of this list gives a drum of 32 inches.
- **The muffle of the bass drum (section 5).** The palm touches the far head near its near rim,
  not near the middle, and the upper body turns 20° to the right for the muffle, because the
  short left arm reaches no farther. The drum keeps its place: a drum moved or turned to the left
  put an upper arm into the body or the beater off its spot. No source gives the turn.
- **The rolls (sections 7 and 8).** A roll of the suspended cymbal strikes at most 12 times each
  second with the two paws in turn (`STRIKE_MOTION.rollSec`, `thinRolls()`). A triangle roll
  strikes at most 6 times each second (`handRollSec`).
- **The hold of the triangle (section 8).** The clip is a little below the mouth
  (`PERCUSSION_PLACES.triangleClip`). A real player holds it near eye level, but the long snout of
  the badger stands there: a clip 0.4 H higher put the claws 5.8 cm into the snout. The paw turns
  90° around its forearm, so that the palm faces the clip (`triangleTwist`). No source gives the
  turn of the hand.
- **The change of the instrument (section 8).** A switch takes 2.7 s of rest: 0.2 s after the
  note, two moves of 0.45 s, 0.6 s at the table, and the lift of 1 s before the next note
  (`switchRestSec()`). No source gives the time [67]. With a shorter rest, the left paw keeps the
  triangle, and the tip of the beater strikes the tambourine on the table near its rim, as a soft
  fast passage plays on the table [65] [66]. No demo has a rest that long between the triangle and
  the tambourine, so each demo plays the tambourine on the table.
- **The tilt of the held tambourine (section 8).** The head tilts about 23° from level, not 45°:
  a steeper tambourine went into the round belly, and the short right arm did not reach a
  tambourine farther out. The fingers point forward and to the left, so that the right wrist
  stays out of the belly.
- **The triangle stand (section 8).** The triangle hangs from a stand at the left of the player,
  not on the table, so that the left paw hangs it with the turn of its hold. A turn to lay it
  flat on the table twisted the paw 150° in 0.05 s. Between the stand and the tambourine on the
  table, the paw bends about 110° on its forearm in the 0.6 s at the table, with a twist of about
  20° (`tablePaw()`). No source gives the turn of the hand in a change.
- **The rest at the suspended cymbal (section 7).** No source gives it. The mallets lie across the
  body as the snare sticks, above the plate (`SUSPENDED_CYMBAL_HOLD.set`).
- **The muffle of the suspended cymbal (section 7).** The sources give the stop of the hand cymbals
  at the chest, and no grip of a suspended cymbal. The closed paw rests on the edge, and the mallet
  points up from the plate, away from the bell (`EdgeDamp.share`). The rule of the rest is the
  rule of the hand cymbals. A MIDI drum note
  has no written length, so each note before a rest of 0.5 s stops.
- **The hand cymbals (section 7).** The angle of the flam is 15° at each loudness, smaller than
  the 35° to 45° of [60]: a small angle that keeps the plates not parallel [61]. The plates tilt 15° from vertical [16]. No concert source gives the rest place: in a long
  rest, the arms hang with the plates at the sides, as a marching method holds them [24].
- **The xylophone grip (section 9).** The wrists stay about 13 cm over the bars, and the mallets
  slope down to the bars. A real player keeps the hands at most one inch over the keyboard [39]. Each mallet points from a place out from its
  shoulder (`PercussionHold.home`), so that the forearms stay out of the leaning belly. Lower
  wrists put the forearms 2.4 cm to 3.1 cm into the belly. The mallets thus point a little
  further in. No source gives this choice.
- **The rest at the xylophone (section 9).** No source gives it. The mallets lie across the body
  as the snare sticks (`PercussionHold.set`).
- **The lean over the xylophone (section 9).** The spine leans forward over the bars
  (`PercussionHold.lean`), because the short arms do not reach the bars from an upright body. The
  hips keep their place, because a small move of the hips tilts the short legs far.
- **The size of the bars (section 9).** The bars are smaller than real bars (`XYLOPHONE`), because
  the short arms reach only a narrow band. A part out of the range of the bars moves by octaves to
  the highest octave of the bars (`xylophoneShift()`). No source gives this choice.
- **The reach (section 9).** A bar out of the reach of a paw goes to the other paw
  (`PercussionHold.reach`). Thus some fast pairs stay in one paw. A chromatic run stays mostly
  in one paw, because the paws never pass each other, while a real player alternates the hands
  over the two rows of bars [34].
- **The two depths (section 9).** The left mallet strikes nearer to the player than the middle of
  a bar, and the right mallet farther (`offsetL`, `offsetR`), because the short left arm misses
  the far part of the bars. No source gives a numeric height between strikes.

### The instruments

The models of `percussion.ts` follow these facts of real concert instruments (design 4.6):

- **Timpani.** A Ludwig Grand Symphonic timpano has a copper kettle with a rolled lip, which hangs
  in the suspension ring, a Balanced Action pedal, and pull rods in its struts [46 (a search
  summary only)]. The app gives each kettle a black frame of struts and a pedal at the side of the
  player, as `orchestra-latest.png` shows.
- **Bass drum.** The Yamaha CB-7000 concert bass drums have a birch shell, birch hoops, and 12
  lugs [43 (a search summary only)].
- **Snare drum.** The Ludwig Supraphonic LM402 is 14 by 6.5 inches, with 10 lugs, triple flanged
  hoops, and a throw-off strainer [47 (a search summary only)].
- **Cymbals.** Zildjian orchestral pairs come with a pair of leather straps [48 (a search summary
  only)]. The lathe cuts tonal grooves into a cast cymbal as it spins [49 (a search summary
  only)].
- **Xylophone.** The Musser M50 has rosewood bars from F4 to C8 and aluminum resonators [50 (a
  search summary only)]. A uniform free bar vibrates with its nodes at 0.224 of its length from
  each end [51 (a search summary only)], and the cord and the rails hold the bars there. A
  resonator tube that is closed at the bottom resonates at a quarter of the wavelength of the note
  [52 (a search summary only)]. In air at 343 m/s, this gives 16 cm for C5 and 3 cm for F7
  (derived).
