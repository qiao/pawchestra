# Conductor technique: how a conductor shows the music with the body

This file is the target of the expression of the bear conductor of the app. It describes how a
real conductor shows the dynamics, the articulation, the breath before an entry, the accents, the
climax, and the end of a held chord, with the baton, the left hand, and the body, and how the
conductor bows at the end of a piece. Each fact has a source.

**Upkeep.** This file is current. A change of the motion of the conductor updates section 8 "Use
in the app" and each fact that it finds wrong, in the same commit. Each new fact gets its source.

**Source marks.** Each fact names its source by a number of section 9. "(abstract)" marks a fact
from an abstract only. "(a search summary only)" marks a fact from a search summary, because the
page or the book did not open. "(derived)" marks a rule that this file makes from the facts of its
sources. A fact with no mark comes from the text of the source.

## Terms

- **Ictus.** The point of a beat: the place and the time at which the baton marks the beat.
- **Preparation.** The gesture in the beat before an entry. It gives the tempo, the dynamics, and
  the character of the entry.
- **Click.** A short, sharp stop of the baton at the ictus, made in the wrist.
- **Release.** The gesture that stops the sound. Other names are "cut-off" and "cutoff".
- **Fermata.** A note or a rest that the conductor holds longer than its value.
- **Mirror.** The left hand moves as the image of the baton hand in a mirror.

## 1. Dynamics and the size of the gesture

- "A big gesture is loud and a small one is quiet and that can often be the case" [2].
- A generous beat can also fit quiet music that flows and is positive [2].
- A crescendo and a "poco più forte" get "a slightly larger beat" [1].
- At a change to forte, the beat becomes "more energetic (and not that much bigger)" [1].
- A fast beat travels a shorter distance than a slow beat [1].
- A conductor conducts "very softly in the beginning of a crescendo and loudly in the start of a
  diminuendo", because the players change too soon [2].
- A forte gesture moves toward the players, with jerky motion, high tension, wide motion ("wide
  open arms"), much motion, and an upward direction [6].
- A piano gesture is fluid, with low tension, small amplitude, little motion, and a downward
  direction [6].
- Most gestures stay below the chin. A beat above the head makes the conductor "a moving
  target" [4].

## 2. The left hand

- The left hand mirrors the baton, gives cues, and shapes a crescendo or a diminuendo [5].
- "Most textbooks will tell you not to mirror – but all the great conductors do it" [5].
- Palms up ask for forte, and palms down ask for piano [6].
- For piano, both open hands with the palms down move a little down, as if they keep a surface
  down [6].
- For a crescendo, the arms with open hands curve out while the shoulders rise [6]. The open left
  hand also turns forward in wider and wider rounds [6].
- For a diminuendo, the left arm with an open hand, palm down, moves back [6].
- A raised palm up shows a sustained sound. A palm up is for a hand that stays still [7].
- "The left hand smoothly descending" helps to lower the intensity [7].
- The left hand mirrors outside of the torso while the right hand beats [7].

## 3. The breath and the preparation

- One preparatory gesture is enough for an entry [1].
- The breath of the conductor "should not be audible ... but it should be visible" [1].
- The preparation shows the ensemble "how to breathe for the entrance" [8].
- When the conductor breathes with the ensemble, an accurate entrance is more probable [8].
- Breathing gives confidence to the players when the hands do very little [2].
- A sudden change of the dynamics shows in the beat before the change [2].

## 4. Legato and staccato

- A legato beat has "no 'click' in the beat, just a change of direction" [1].
- For staccato, "the beat takes place in the wrist to give a short click for each beat" [1].
- The Grammar of Conducting has separate beat types for legato, light staccato, full staccato,
  and marcato, and a section on the change from legato to staccato (a search summary only) [10].
- A forte can be a jerky motion "with its sudden blocked impact", as the fists of Bernstein in a
  rehearsal of The Rite of Spring [6].

## 5. Accents and sudden changes

- For a sforzato, the conductor suddenly closes the fist [6].
- A conductor does not show a forte or an sfp with an extra gesture on its own beat. The gesture
  of the beat before shows it, and it is "more energetic rather than bigger" [2].

## 6. The body

- For a softer sound, the conductor makes the body smaller: "Bust forward, shoulders closed, head
  forward downward" [6].
- For a crescendo, the shoulders rise with the open arms [6].
- Conductors sometimes bend the knees and drop a little to ask for a softer sound. The reverse
  motion asks for a louder sound (a search summary only) [11].
- Knees that bob with the metre were "the biggest obstacle to sustaining the note" [9].
- Knees that bend too much make the beats "start and finish in different places" [4].
- A crouch is a deliberate gesture for a special moment [4].
- "We should aim to be as grounded as possible" (Mark Elder) [4].
- Observers see the expression of a conductor in the kinematics of the beat, and the amplitude of
  the motion can show the size of the expression (abstract) [12].

## 7. The fermata, the release, and the bow

- On a fermata, "the conductor just waits on the fermata" [3].
- The left hand cuts off the sound, and "the right hand does nothing" [3].
- A circle can release the sound: clockwise for the left hand and counterclockwise for the right
  hand. The circle "begins and ends at 6:00, or the bottom of the circle" [8].
- A close of the fingers of the left hand can also release the sound. The release needs "a clear
  point of cessation of the music" [8].
- The release gives "the character required of the final note" [8].
- At the end of a piece, the conductor "turns around to bow and acknowledge the applause of the
  audience", and so accepts the applause "on behalf of the ensemble" [13].
- The players bring up the next piece "only after the director has turned back to face the
  ensemble" [13]. Thus the conductor turns back to the players after the bow.
- A bow is a bend "forward from the waist, keeping your back straight", with the feet together
  [14].
- "Look down as you bow, never up at the audience" [14].
- A performer holds the instrument in a "natural and casual hold" in the bow, and the bow "should
  not call attention to it" [14].
- A bow after a short piece or a small applause is partial and "quite short". A long applause gets
  a low bow, held "for several counts" [14].
- No conducting source of section 9 describes the feet in the turn to the audience. A study of
  standing turns of 180° gives the motion of a healthy person [15]. "Segment reorientation began
  with the head followed by the rotation of the trunk and pelvis, then the lead and trail feet",
  at each turn speed [15].
- In a fast turn, younger adults started the head at 0.52 s, the pelvis at 0.57 s, the lead foot
  at 0.69 s, and the trail foot at 0.89 s after the signal [15].
- Younger adults took 2.95 steps in a fast turn and 3.43 steps in a moderate turn, in 1.72 s and
  2.22 s of stepping. Each step turned the foot by about 75° to 80° [15].
- The peak separation of the head from the pelvis was about 22° in a fast turn [15].

## 7a. The baton in the hand

- "You hold the baton with your thumb and index finger", "the middle finger slightly closed but
  loose", and "the thumb slightly forward" [16].
- "The end lightly touches the palm of the hand but is not blocked" [16].
- The baton is "as if it were a continuation of his arm, a fourth part" [16].
- "Think of the baton as an extension of your lower arm", with "a sense of line going from inside
  your elbow, through the arm and hand, into the baton" [17].
- "It is important to avoid angles between lower arm and baton", and to avoid "gripping the
  baton too tight or too loose" [17].
- Max Rudolf holds the baton with the thumb and the first and second fingers, with the butt
  against the palm (a search summary only) [10].
- No source of section 9 gives the hold of the baton with the arms down. The hand keeps the grip
  of the play, because the baton does not move in the hand (derived).

## 8. Use in the app

The bear conductor follows these rules (design 5.4). `pose/conductor-expression.ts` reads the
expression from the score before the play (`buildExpressionTrack()`), and the pose looks it up at
each frame (`expressionAt()`). The values are in `EXPRESSION_RULES` and `EXPRESSION_MOTION`.

- **Dynamics (sections 1, 6).** The level of each beat is the mean level of the time around it.
  A loud passage makes the beat pattern a little larger and higher (`loudSize`, `loudLift`), and
  its staccato stroke more energetic: a longer stop at the top and a faster fall into the ictus
  (`loudEnergy`), as the forte of section 1 is "more energetic" more than larger. The body comes
  up with the head (`loudLean`, `loudHead`). A crescendo grows the beat over the build
  (`riseSize`), opens the chest, and raises the head and the shoulders (`riseLean`, `riseHead`,
  `riseShrug`), as the open arms and the rising shoulders of section 2. A soft passage makes the
  pattern smaller and lower (`softSize`, `softDrop`). The body leans toward the players, the head
  goes down, and the knees bend (`softLean`, `softHead`, `softCrouch`), as the smaller body of
  section 6. The app keeps the beat below the chin, except at a climax (section 1). At beat 1,
  the right wrist is in front of the chest, below the shoulder joint, with a bent elbow
  (`CONDUCTOR_HOLD.beatPoint`). The median of the play is at about the shoulder joint, and the
  largest loud beats reach about the chin. The elbows hang down and out, a little away from the
  body (`poleL`, `poleR`).
- **The baton in the paw (section 7a).** The butt lies in the heel of the palm (`BATON_GRIP` of
  `models/instruments/baton.ts`), and the stick leaves the paw through the palm under the curled
  claws, turned toward the thumb (`STICK_TO_THUMB`, `STICK_TO_PALM`), so that the closed paw
  grips it. The fingers curl around it in the play, at rest, and in the bow
  (`CONDUCTOR_HOLD.batonCurl`). The turn toward the palm also brings the paw near the line of
  the forearm. In the beat, the baton is a longer line of the forearm, a little in, as sources
  16 and 17 give it: the baton points 12° in at beat 1 (`batonPivot`), the arm reaches out to
  the tip (`beatPoint`), and the elbow points out to the side (`poleR`). The baton stands about
  32° off the forearm (median of the play), and nearly all of that angle is inward. A fist of a
  bear paw holds the baton at an angle to the paw, and a baton nearer to straight forward touched
  the desk of the stand after a final chord, so the app does not reach a straight line.
- **The rest pose.** Before the start and after the bow, the arms hang relaxed beside the body,
  and the baton hangs down beside the leg, about 17° forward of the vertical
  (`CONDUCTOR_HOLD.restBaton`), as the closed hand of section 7a hangs with the arm (derived).
  No source of section 9 gives the rest place. The paw turns with no twist on its forearm, so the
  baton does not reach the vertical. In the lift, the paw turns early toward the paw of the beat
  (`LIFT_HAND_EASE`), so that the tip points forward and then forward and up, as the baton
  continues the line of the forearm of section 7a (derived).
- **The left hand (section 2).** The left hand rises open in a crescendo and comes down open in a
  diminuendo (`LEFT_HAND_PLACES`, `conductor-left.ts`), and it mirrors the beat in a loud
  passage, outside of the torso (`MIRROR_IN`), in the plane of the right hand. Between its
  gestures, it rests low at the left of the lower chest (`LEFT_HAND_PLACES.quiet`), so that the
  gestures stay below the chin (section 1, derived). In a soft passage, it takes the lowered open palm
  (`softPalm`). The paw turns its palm up in a crescendo, on a held chord, and at a climax, and
  down in a diminuendo and a soft passage (`LEFT_HAND_PLACES`, `palm`), as the palms of section 2.
  The paw twists on its forearm for this turn (`PALM_TWIST`), as the forearm of a conductor
  turns the palm. The twist stops at `MAX_TWIST_L`, so the palm turns only a part of the way.
  The lowered palm points forward near the line of the forearm, and the places of the left hand
  turn with the upper body to each side, so that the arm does not reach back across the chest.
  A cue extends the arm nearly straight to the section (derived).
- **The breath (section 3).** The chest and the head rise in the preparation before the first
  beat, before each phrase start after a time with no note start, and before each accent
  (`breathLean`, `breathHead`), and the shoulders rise (`breathShrug`). They come down after the
  entry (`breathReleaseSec`). Two breaths are at least `breathGapSec` apart.
- **Legato and staccato (section 4).** Each stroke reads the mean length of its notes against the
  beat. A legato stroke moves with no stop, as a change of direction. A staccato or accented
  stroke rebounds fast to the top of its arc, stops there, and falls into the next ictus with its
  full speed (`strokeOf()` of `conductor-notes.ts`): the click of section 4. The app makes the
  click with the whole stroke, because the wrist of the bear follows the baton.
- **Accents (section 5).** An accent is a strong onset well above the onsets of the 2 s before it.
  The head, the upper body, and the shoulders jolt on it in a short pulse (`joltHead`, `joltLean`,
  `joltShrug`), and the knees dip on an accented downbeat (`dip`). Of two accents within
  `accentGapSec`, the larger stays, so the head does not jolt on each chord of a run. The breath
  before it is the gesture of the beat before. The knees do not bob on each beat, because section 6
  names that as a fault.
- **The stance (section 6).** The upper body does not sway to the side with the beat or the bar,
  and the hips stay over the feet: the grounded stance of section 6. A side sway with the bar,
  derived from the fluid piano gestures of section 1, read as a metronome on the bear, so the app
  has none. For a cue, the chest twists further toward the section (`cueTwist`), and a left cue
  takes away a part of a forward lean (`cueUpright`).
- **The climax (sections 1, 2, 6).** A climax is a loud downbeat after a rise of the level, at
  least `climaxStartSec` into the piece. Only the largest rises stay: one for each
  `climaxEverySec`, at least `climaxGapSec` apart. Both arms open wide and high for one bar: the
  baton goes out and up (`climaxOut`, `climaxUp`) and keeps a part of its beat, and the left hand
  opens with the palm up (`LEFT_HAND_PLACES.open`). The chest opens, the head lifts, and the
  shoulders rise (`climaxLean`, `climaxHead`, `climaxShrug`).
- **The final chord (section 7).** The app finds the last chord of at least `finalShare` of the
  seats that sounds to the end of the sound. A chord shorter than `finalMinSec` gets no hold: the
  baton stops at its beat with no rebound, and the left hand closes. The baton leaves the beat and
  holds above the beat of the chord, and it rises slowly to sustain the sound. The left palm holds
  the chord up. Before the end, the baton makes one counterclockwise circle that starts and ends at
  its bottom, and the left hand closes at the end. Then the baton stops a little lower
  (`finalCircle`, `finalClose`). The right hand moves in the release of the app too, because the
  release of the last chord of a piece ends the piece, and the bow follows it.
- **The end of the piece (section 7).** After the cut-off, the bear holds the release in the
  silence, and lowers the arms to the sides (`END_BOW`, `pose/end-bow.ts`). Then it turns on the
  podium to the audience with steps, bows from the waist with the eyes down, and turns back
  to the orchestra, as the conductor of source 13. The baton hangs in the right paw, as the
  natural hold of the instrument of source 14. The bow is partial, 35°, and short (about 2 s down
  and up), because a conductor bows for the orchestra and the app has no applause (derived from
  14). No source gives the times of the silence and the lowering (derived). The whole
  end takes `END_BOW_SEC` (7.4 s), and the end card shows after it. A play during the bow turns
  the bear back from where it is with steps, and the arms lift only when it nearly faces the
  orchestra (`transportEndMotion()`, derived).
- **The steps of the turn (section 7, source 15).** The turn follows the fast standing turn of
  source 15 (`TURN_STEPS`). The head starts first and leads the hips by about 20°
  (`headLeadSec`), the hips start 0.12 s before the lead foot (`hipsLeadSec`), and the left foot
  leads the turn to the left. The half turn takes three steps and a small step that closes the
  feet, in about 1.4 s of stepping (`pitchSec`, `swingSec`), near the 2.95 steps in 1.72 s of source 15.
  Each step turns a foot by 60° to 120° (`gap`), near the 75° to 80° of source 15. A foot moves
  only in the air, so no planted foot slides or turns. Source 15 does not give the weight shift,
  the knee bend, or the lift of the foot (`weight`, `knees`, `lift`, derived).
- **Where the app differs.** The app shows an accent with the jolt, the dip, and the stroke, and
  the left hand does not close to a fist for a sforzato (section 5). The left hand does not turn in
  wider rounds for a crescendo (section 2). It rises open.
